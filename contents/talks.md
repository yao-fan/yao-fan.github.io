<summary><strong>Yao Fan</strong>. "From Marx to Rawls and Beyond: Or, How I Learnt to Stop Worrying and Love 'Bourgeois' Theories of Justice." Bridgewater Associates Internal Conference, United States (March 2024). <a href="static/assets/From-Marx-to-Rawls-and-Beyond.pdf">Handout</a></summary>
<p><strong>Abstract.</strong> A common line among Marxist political theorists is that justice is part of the ideology of capitalism, invented by the ruling class, to exploit and fool the educated bourgeoisie (the middle class). This idea originates from Karl Marx himself, who left several controversial and confusing remarks about justice in writing. At some places, Marx suggests that capitalism is robbery. At other places, he says that capitalism is just. Together he seems to be suggesting that robbery is just.</p>
<p>In this talk, I will explain what these controversial remarks are about. I will first introduce Marx's theory of exploitation (in its modern form) to make sense of the claim that "capitalism is robbery." I will explain why the theory of exploitation is not an adequate condemnation of capitalism and why Marx needs a theory of justice. Then I will move on to the discussion of justice, explaining senses in which capitalism is just and in which it is not. In a nutshell, capitalism can be transactionally just but structurally unjust.</p>

<summary><strong>Yao Fan</strong>. "Science and Commonsense: An Inferentialist Approach toward Primitivism of Colors." 12th Arche Graduate Conference, University of St Andrews, United Kingdom (February 2021). <a href="static/assets/Science-and-Commonsense-Color-Slides.pdf">Slides (PDF)</a></summary>
<p><strong>Abstract.</strong> In his recent book Primitive Colors (2017), Joshua Gert defended a primitivism about colors. By adapting Benacerraf’s argument against the reduction of numbers to sets, Gert provides an argument for the ontological irreducibility of colors without relying on their explanatory autonomy, which makes Gert’s defense quite a novel one. In this paper, I provide an alternative argument in defense of the irreducibility of colors, which avoids certain difficulties faced by Gert’s account. My argument relies on what I call “the autonomy of color judgments”, which asserts that all propositions that are direct reasons for/against color judgments are situated in the manifest image of the world. For any color judgment, once I have a firm grasp of those propositions in the manifest image that are directly connected with this color judgment under the reason for/against relation, I can make a final verdict on the truth or the falsity of this color judgment and no further scientific discovery can convince me otherwise.</p>

<summary><strong>Yao Fan</strong>. "From General Logic to Transcendental Logic." Pittsburgh Area Kant Colloquium, United States (October 2020). <a href="static/assets/From-General-Logic-to-Transcendental-Logic-Draft.docx">Draft (DOCX)</a></summary>
<p><strong>Abstract.</strong> Tolley (2012) proposed an account of the difference between Kant’s transcendental logic and traditional general logic, arguing that transcendental logic concerns a different aspect of understanding, namely, the content of thinking and judging, albeit in a very abstract manner. Tolley claims that his view refutes the orthodox view that transcendental logic is different from general logic by having a different domain of application. This talk contrasts Tolley’s view with what I call “the objective account”, which is a particular version of “domain-subordinative” accounts of transcendental logic Tolley tries to reject. The objective account contends that transcendental logic concerns all thoughts/judgments of objects, in contrast to general logic, which concerns all thoughts/judgments simpliciter.</p>
